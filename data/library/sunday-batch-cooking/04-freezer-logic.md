# Lesson 4: Freezer logic

## Freeze components, not finished plates

Finished plates rarely freeze well, because each part of the plate wants something different. Rice dries out, salad goes limp and sauce goes watery. Components freeze much better on their own: a bag of cooked lentils, a box of tomato sauce, portions of soup, cooked grains spread flat in a bag.

## The flat bag method

Freeze sauces, soups and grains in zip bags laid flat on a tray. Once they are frozen, stand them up like books on a shelf. Flat bags thaw faster, stack neatly and let you see everything at once. A freezer full of square tubs is a freezer you can't see into.

## The freezer ledger

Every freezer needs a ledger: a sheet of paper on the freezer door listing what went in and when. Write it when you freeze something, and cross it out when you take it out. The ledger stops the slow pile-up of mystery boxes, and it turns your freezer into a menu you can read in ten seconds on a tired evening.

I keep a pencil tied to mine with string. If the pencil isn't there, the ledger doesn't get written.

## Label like you will forget

Label every bag with what it is, the date and how it wants to be reheated. "Tomato sauce, 12 Oct, heat from frozen in a pan" is a label. "Sauce" is a guess you will regret in six weeks.

For cooling, storing and reheating times, follow the food safety guidance where you live. I teach the system, not the rules.
