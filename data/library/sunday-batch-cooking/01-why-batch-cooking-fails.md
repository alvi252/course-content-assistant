# Lesson 1: Why batch cooking usually fails

## The five-container trap

Most people start batch cooking by making one big dish and splitting it into five identical containers. By Wednesday they are bored, by Thursday the containers are pushed to the back of the fridge, and by Friday they order takeaway and decide batch cooking isn't for them. The problem was never the cooking. The problem was eating the same plate five times.

In this course we do something different. We cook components, not meals. A pot of grains, a tray of roasted vegetables, one protein and one sauce can become four different dinners, because you put them together differently each night.

## The 90-minute Sunday

Batch cooking does not need your whole weekend. My rule is the 90-minute Sunday: one block of ninety minutes, timer on, and you stop when it rings. Whatever is cooked by then is your week. Whatever is not cooked waits for next Sunday.

The timer matters more than the recipes. Without a limit, a cooking session grows into a four-hour project, and a four-hour project is something you will skip the moment your Sunday gets busy. Ninety minutes is short enough to protect and long enough to cook three or four components, as long as the oven and the hob are working at the same time.

## Start with two nights, not five

If you are new to this, plan two dinners, not five. Two nights of food you are actually happy to eat teach you more than five nights of food you abandon. Add a third night only when two feels easy.

Most of my students settle at three or four planned dinners and leave the rest of the week open for leftovers, invitations and the nights when you just want toast. An unplanned night is not a failed plan. It is part of the plan.
